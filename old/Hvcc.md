### macro essay
Should unemployment benefits automatically increase during recessions?

Automatic fiscal stabilizers are necessary to conduct efficient and effective fiscal policy in response to large scale crisis in modern economies. The economy experiences large transitory costs when the government does not decisively intervene in recessions, unnecessarily prolonging suffering. Overall, this is because the effect of "animal spirits" are powerful; even when production realities have not changed significantly, aggregate demand may suddenly fall, resulting in production below potential. We consider first potential counterarguments against fiscal stimulus during a crisis, then compare effective methods of injecting fiscal stimulus, and conclude that UI should automatically increase during recessions, and that a phased in model should be adopted.

Government borrowing may cause the crowding out effect in financial markets. Because the demand for capital increases, interest rates, especially in the longer term, rise, displacing and discouraging private borrowing and investment, offsetting the stimulus. Interest rates can also rise in the short term if fiscal stimulus fights monetary policy, which may raise interest rates to control an overheating economy. It does not really apply, however, when interest rates are stuck near zero, so fiscal stimulus is highly effective here (CBPP). Open capital flows also mean government spending that results in higher domestic interest rates attracts foreign capital, increasing demand for currency used to buy domestic assets. As the currency appreciates, exports fall and imports rise, reducing gross domestic production. This is however, a small effect for large economies like the US. 

If the crowding out effect is fully observed, that government spending necessarily causes an economy to overheat or that simply diverts resources from the private sector, recessions would not be prolonged, or occur in the first place. This is because perfect crowding out would suggest the economy is always operating at its maximum productive capacity, in this neoclassical model. The neoclassical model suggests when wages decline, demand for labor increases proportionately, bringing it back up. It also suggests when aggregate demand falls, prices decrease, bringing back demand.

However in reality, aggregate demand may resemble a doom loop downwards, as unemployment instead results in further decreases in aggregate demand, that multiplies, needlessly grinding the economy to a halt. This happens when equilibrium prices are lower than the price at the shutdown condition for more firms, resulting in more layoffs. Sticky wages and prices explain that while adjustments may occur in the long run, in the short run, millions may become unemployed and suffer, with needless unemployment that reflects the large transitory cost of an economy failing to work at full capacity. This is because wages are usually fixed and cannot be instantly repriced, that cutting wages can hurt morale and retention, so instead layoffs are more common than repricing, or that employees refuse to accept wage cuts, and instead leave. In other words, the real economy is somewhere between the neoclassical zone of low aggregate supply elasticity and the Keynesian zone of high aggregate supply elasticity, indicating high transitory costs.

Ricardian equivalence is another statement of the crowding out effect, suggesting that fiscal stimulus fails, because government expenditure has to be financed ultimately through taxation. Stimulus only postpones the bill that has to be repaid for the taxpayer and the macroeconomy. However, several unrealistic conditions must hold for this to be true (Bernheim). Namely, that capital markets must be perfect, families are perfectly altruistic, and that the path of government policy is fixed. Capital markets are imperfect: Banks are unable to enforce a potential claim on future labor income, which is bad collateral, and banks are unable to trust their credit perfectly. As such, households cannot borrow based on future labor, or only at worse rates using credit cards and personal loans, rather than the hypothetical interest rate. Next, Ricardian equivalence requires altruistic families. This condition again does not hold. The theory supposes savings from the current generation increase in anticipation of future debt repayment, which may only be the responsibility of those 2 or more generations later, or even never. The reality is government spending today does not compel benefeciaries today to save more for their great-great-great-grandchildren, and that government tax policy may have completely changed by then. 

The failure of these imperfect conditions in practice explain why Ricardian equivalence is almost never observed, with a savings offset near 0.5 (Bernheim), rather than a 1:1 increase in savings with government stimulus. This justifies fiscal stimulus: the government is not liquidity constrained in the same way, paying usually the lowest interest rate, with its credit so trustworthy and scale so large US bond rates are literally how the risk-free interest rate is usually defined globally. Government bonds represent the credit of a whole country and its institutions, the claim on its large future tax revenues, and its coercive power to tax regardless of external factors. As such, fiscal stimulus, especially during crisis where real production is indeed lower than potential output, may theoretically be the less distortionary and more free market outcome, fixing the market failures that result from liquidity constraints.

Improbable conditions must hold for stimulus to fail unequivocally in the short term, as the neoclassical model implies. The theoretical model incorrectly neglects to consider the human condition: present bias, the imperfect decisions that must be made with imperfect information, resulting in imperfect outcomes, and the effect of "animal spirits", where consumer and business confidence materially affects outcomes. Regardless of the exact failing of the theoretical model assuming perfectly rational behavior, empirical observation shows stimulus can indeed result in significant fiscal multipliers. SNAP, the supplemental nutrition assistance program, and UI, both have high fiscal multipliers of 1.5, directly increasing short term aggregate demand. Establishing that fiscal stimulus is necessary, we now consider which form is most effective.

First, unemployment insurance, as in the question. UI has a very high fiscal multiplier of 1.6 (CBPP), but has a distortionary moral hazard cost, of discouraging work. The figures for how much pandemic UI held back aggregate employment recovery are 3.4% between April and December 2020 (Hornstein), and that a 10% increase in benefit levels generates a large 13-17% increase in unemployment, versus only a 2% increase from a 10% increase in benefit duration (Galecka-Burdziak). Nearly 60% of observed moral hazard is from the liquidity effect, that higher generosity affords people the opportunity to find a satisfactory match, rather than being rushed into work, being concentrated in low-asset households. It is also an automatic stabilizer, with inherently increased spending when unemployment spikes, similar to Medicare and SNAP.

Second, are cash based transfers common during the pandemic. These are less distortionary than UI, as they are unconditional, but have weak fiscal multiplier effects, closer to 0.6 (CBPP). Higher income or still employed households may primarily save the cash, or pay down debts, rather than immediately spending them and allowing it to recirculate in the economy. According to Coibion's survey, individuals planned to spend 40% of the transfer on average, that in the end, 42% was spent, 27% saved and 31% used on paying debts. Verifying the effect of wealth on whether the stimulus is immediately spent, 77% of respondents earning less than 25000$ immediately used the stimulus to pay for everyday expenses like rent and food.

Ultimately, a mix of both must be employed, despite the inevitable tradeoff between means testing allowing for more effective stimulus and potential distortionary impact. Automatic stabilizers like UI, Medicare and SNAP are essential for their direct effectiveness, and should be increased during recessions. Benefits for Medicare, SNAP, and for example the EITC can also be tapered off to prevent benefit cliffs, helping neutralize the distortionary impact. Furthermore, UI is shown to generally be minimally distortionary in practice as long as the supplement is less than the potential income gained from work. This is especially true when considering that any apparent increase in unemployment from UI may simply represent individuals not being rushed into an unsatisfactory job or one with poor pay. Broad based stimulus checks are still essential, however (Sahm). During recessions, consumer purchasing sentiment decreases across the board and follow closely in tandem, regardless of the household income quartile. A big cause of recessions is indeed poor "animal spirits", which broad based lump-sum checks are especially good at improving, and especially in a non-distortionary way.

Regardless of the form of stimulus, automatic stabilizers are generally preferable to legislative discretion. Requiring the full political process every recession is impractical, and delays stimulus when it is most necessary: at the beginning, where the economy has the most slack. Furthermore, automatic stimulus may help calm pessimistic "animal spirits", with the economy expecting government intervention that would restore normalcy, making the aggregate demand doom loop less likely. There is also an accurate predictor of recessions (and others too) that would allow automatic stimulus checks to be effectively contingent on a true recession (Sahm). The Sahm rule states that a recession has occured when the 3 month moving average of unemployment is 0.5% above its low over the prior 12 months. This rule has been highly effective with few false positives, and can reliably deliver stimulus early in the recession. Combine this indicator with decisive lump sum stimulus checks worth, for example, 0.7% of GDP (Sahm) every recession year, scaled with a formula until unemployment is no more than 2% more than the prerecession rate; (current unemployment-prerecession unemployment-2) / (peak unemployment-prerecession unemployment-2) * 0.7% of GDP. In 2008, where stimulus was indecisive early on and failed to vigorously suppress unemployment, this would have kept stimulus high for 3 years before scaling down only later, rather than a delayed legislative realization that stimulus was insufficient, that led to increased stimulus only late in the recession after a large initial tranch. 

In conclusion, recessions may indeed naturally end in "the long term", but as Keynes has said, in the long run we are all dead. Aggregate supply, due to sticky wages and prices, the shutdown condition, and the aggregate demand doom loop caused by unemployment, is somewhat elastic, especially in the near term. Fiscal stimulus must be used countercyclically to prevent needless suffering and wasted production potential. This is best done partly through means targeting that should strive to be minimally distortionary. For example, unemployment benefits and SNAP should be boosted automatically when a recession is detected, possibly using the Sahm rule, in addition to tapering social safety net benefits that also function as automatic stabilizers. This should be combined with decisive and broad-based lump sum stimulus checks that bring back consumer optimism.
