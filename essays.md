A time I felt left out when I was alone in the fish tank. It is so named because of the thick glass blocks, instead of a thin glass window, that made up its exterior facade, that refracted light such that those inside looked like small fish. Apt, as I felt different from those outside. I would probably have been doing physics, which I was doing for a large chunk of my free time in 7th grade. During the prior long end of schol break, I was bored, somehow, and maybe numbed by video games. I always liked math and science, and decided to look at what I would be learning the next few years. Biology I did, for about 2 weeks, then chemistry for another, then I moved on to physics. It might have been the mathematical nature of it, or how it's especially fundamental, but I decided it was my favorite. In the fish tank, I didn't feel isolated; I had good friends, just not those who were especially interested in physics. The fad then was student leadership. I had nothing wrong with that, but the rat race seemed pointless: applicants outnumbered seats, and most were more than qualified. It seemed daunting. So I filled up half a dozen notebooks that year, got to know the school secretary particularly well, printing out thousand page books during recess. (I realized printing there was a multiple cheaper than both buying the book, and printing it myself.) Again, after a while I realized belonging does not require being the same, but only an openness that different people can enjoy different things. The real test came when I moved to the US before my 9th grade year. I was already aware, with no offense meant, of the mediocre school I would be going to in comparison. I got along well with my classmates, made good friends, but the main point of frustration was having to practically repeat the physics and calculus, which I had already self studied. This was, in my view, due to bureaucratic NY public school requirements. There was no physical fish tank around me anymore, but I still felt isolated doing my own work, usually physics, ironically during that same subject class. I got along decently with my physics teacher, but there was mostly nothing she could do. It is tempting at this point to blame purely district policy for this injustice, but frankly, I would have faced the same exact problem entering 9th and 10th grade in Singapore. I realized too in math and science classes then in Singapore, I didn't feel particularly challenged either, even if I wasn't directly repeating amterial. Here, I felt most isolated, with few like-minded peers, but I learned to accept the circumstances. I would continue my self study, became an active member of the school's science olympiad club, and also registered for dual-enrolled courses at the local community college. This is also when I became more interested in economics and public policy. Just like in physics, models are imperfect approximations of reality, and this forced me to expand my model of real life. In the end, I realized no circumstances are perfect, but that what matters is our ability to adapt, rather than to resign ourselves.

The biggest challenge 20 years from now is adapting to AI. In the medium term, the AI bubble in stock markets may pop, causing a significant slow to the real economy, which has AI related capital spending representing almost half of nominal growth. This is combined with the wealth effect denting consumer spending from retail traders who are heavily invested in US markets. Opposition to building data centers in the US seems likely to continue, but overall, AI is likely to advance, even after a pop. Geopolitical competition might make this seem an existential fight, creating a shock in US politics. In the longer term, a question is whether large AI labs would be like robber barons, oligopolizing the technology, though this would probably not be a problem with antitrust regulation. The most important question is whether AI would fundamentally change the role of labor: labor for most of industrial history, even with technology, has taken up a similar share of contribution to production, suggesting technology complements rather than replaces labor. If AI is superhumanly intelligent, the contribution of capital may well swell, exacerbating inequality and prompting a political backlash. However, this would require robotics to also advance in that 20 year timeframe, which seems unlikely; technology usually matures 30 years after the first time "this will change the world in 5 years" is said. Regardless, the result may be seismic for the white collar and service based US economy, leading to huge transition costs and unemployment as people struggle to reskill. I predict a transition to a interpersonal and relationship economy, that rests on what AI cannot replace: emotion and sentience, but the only insight here is that no one knows what will emerge.
